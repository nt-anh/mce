Counting heat, pixel by pixel

When gadolinium is magnetised it warms up and hands heat to the water around it. A near-infrared camera can't see heat directly — but every pixel's absorbance change tells us how much warmer the water column behind it got. Add up all the pixels and you get the total energy, Q, in joules.

Based on Nguyen, Kakuta, Uchida & Nagano, J. Appl. Phys. 135 (2024).

How much heat did the sample give the water?

A gadolinium block sits at the bottom of a quartz cuvette filled with water. A magnet slides on: the Gd warms and heat flows into the water above. The magnet slides off: the Gd cools below the water and pulls heat back.

NIR light crosses the cuvette along the depth y (thickness d). The camera records a flat image of the absorbance change ΔA(x, z), where warmer water absorbs a little more.

Q is the extra heat stored in the water compared with the moment before the magnet arrived. Positive means the water gained heat; negative means the sample took it back.

In plain words: every bit of water that got warmer holds a little extra energy: (density × heat capacity × temperature rise × volume). Q adds all of it up.

The camera squashes the depth for us

Each pixel looks along one straight line through the water (that's what the telecentric lens is for). The absorbance it records is the sum of the absorption changes along that line.

Since Δμ = α ΔT, the pixel's ΔA divided by α is exactly the temperature summed through the depth. That is all we need for energy — we never have to know where along y the warm water sits.

The heat behind one pixel

One pixel sees a thin column of water: its footprint on the sample is σ = (object-side pixel size)², and it runs the full depth d.

Object-side pixel size = camera pixel pitch ÷ lens magnification. For the Gd experiment: 15 µm ÷ 0.36 ≈ 42 µm, so "about 40 µm" is right and σ ≈ 0.0016 mm².

Heat in that column = ρc × σ × (ΔA / α). Notice that d drops out — it's already inside ΔA.

ρc of water = 4.18 J cm⁻³ K⁻¹ = 4.18 × 10⁻³ J mm⁻³ K⁻¹; α = 2.76 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹ mm⁻¹ at 1150 nm; d = 10 mm.

Sum over the pixels that see water

Do the one-pixel calculation for every pixel (i, j) and add. Because ρ, c, σ and α are the same everywhere, you can pull them out and just sum the ΔA values.

Important: only sum the region of interest — pixels that look through water. The sample is opaque, so its pixels have almost no light and meaningless ΔA. Pixels outside the cuvette add only noise.

Default check: 10 × 10 mm of water, 10 mm deep, warmed by 0.5 K on average → ΔA = 2.76 × 10⁻⁴ × 10 × 0.5 = 1.38 × 10⁻³ → Q = 4.18 × 10⁻³ × 1000 mm³ × 0.5 K = 2.09 J. Both routes agree.

Assumptions behind the formula

  • ρ, c and α are constant over the small temperature change (a few K) — reasonable for water.
  • ΔA and α use the same logarithm (base 10), and α is the value for the filter actually used.
  • Water fills the whole path d behind every pixel summed, and the light is parallel (telecentric).
  • Q is the heat in the imaged water at that moment, relative to the reference image. Heat that has gone into the cuvette walls, the air or outside the field of view isn't counted — so it's the sample's heat transfer only while those losses are small.
  • Sign matters: after demagnetisation ΔA goes negative, so Q goes negative — the sample is taking heat back.
